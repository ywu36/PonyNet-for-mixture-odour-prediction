O1: receptor-competition model for mixture distance

Each molecule is mapped to how strongly it binds and activates a set of latent receptors. The molecules of a mixture compete for those receptors, and two mixtures are compared by their receptor patterns. The encoder can be open-POM or MIST: both give a per-molecule vector, and the rest of O1 is the same.

O1.0, the first and simplest step, is drawn in its own section below.

Hidden layer: definition

What it isA layer of R latent receptors (R = 64 to start). It sits between the per-molecule encoder and the distance.
Per molecule m, receptor jbinding x(m, j) = softplus(W_x h(m) + b_x)_j ≥ 0 (how strongly m binds j at its concentration) and efficacy e(m, j) = sigmoid(W_e h(m) + b_e)_j ∈ [0,1] (how strongly m activates j once bound). h(m) is the frozen encoder vector (open-POM 256 numbers or MIST 512).
Hidden state of a mixture Ar_j(A) = Σ_(m∈A) x(m, j)·e(m, j) / (1 + Σ_(m∈A) x(m, j)), j = 1…R, so r(A) ∈ [0,1)^R. For a single molecule m: r_j = x(m, j)·e(m, j) / (1 + x(m, j)).
Hill form (PLAN.md)The binding is written x(m, j) = (c(m) / K(m, j))^n(j), with c(m) the concentration of m, K(m, j) the binding constant and n(j) the Hill coefficient of receptor j; this is the x(m, j) used above. One molecule alone gives r_j = e·c^n / (c^n + K^n), the Hill S-curve of Intensity '25 (Imax ↔ e, EC50 ↔ K). With more molecules, the shared denominator adds competition. Caveat: DREAM 2 gives no concentrations (the mixtures are intensity-matched), so c, K and n collapse into one number per molecule and receptor, and n cannot be learned from DREAM 2 alone. Fix n = 1 there; learn n only with concentration data (e.g. Intensity '25).
What shapes it (no receptor data)① the competitive-binding form itself; ② the distance loss on mixture pairs; ③ an odour-word readout sigmoid(U r) trained on 4,983 single molecules; ④ L1 on x, so each molecule drives few receptors; ⑤ optional: M2OR receptor labels on some units.
What it is notNot a free embedding: POMMix's mixture vector (96-d attention output) has no physical form, and D2Smell has no hidden layer (pair features go into trees). The units are receptor-like; they are not identified with real receptors unless M2OR supervision works.

Hidden layer: how it works

given (input)Two mixtures A and B, each a set of molecules (SMILES), e.g. A = {m1, m2}, B = {m3}. No concentrations (the mixtures are intensity-matched).
target (output)d(A, B) in [0, 1]: the human perceptual distance between the two mixtures. Training labels: the 820 DREAM 2 training pairs only (Bushdid, Snitz 1/2, Ravia 1/2/4/5/6, Metamer). No D2Smell augmentation and no extra POMMix pairs.
also given in trainingOdour words of 4,983 single molecules (GS-LF), used only by the readout loss.

Operations used in the equations

operationdefinitionshapeintuition and why we use it here
linear map W h + bz_j = Σ_(k=1..D) W[j, k]·h_k(m) + b_jIndices: j = receptor unit, k = feature of h(m). Row j of W is receptor j's template over the molecule features. The dot product scores how well molecule m matches that template: high score → binds (or activates) strongly. One score per receptor. This is the only place the molecule's features enter.
softplussoftplus(z) = ln(1 + e^z)A smooth ramp: ≈ 0 for very negative z, ≈ z for large z, always > 0. Why for binding x: x must be ≥ 0 (a molecule cannot hold negative sites) and has no upper limit (x = 100 means it takes almost every site). For negative z, softplus ≈ e^z, which is the physical form x = e^(−ΔG/kT) for weak binders. For large z it grows linearly instead of exponentially, so training does not blow up. ReLU would give exactly 0 with zero gradient, so a unit could stop learning; exp could overflow.
sigmoidsigmoid(z) = 1 / (1 + e^(−z))Squashes any number into (0, 1), smoothly. Why for efficacy e: e is a fraction of the receptor's maximum activation, so it must lie between 0 (blocker) and 1 (full activator). Why for the distance d: the target distance lies in [0, 1].
weighted Euclidean√( Σ_j w_j² (r_j(A) − r_j(B))² )Length of the difference between the two receptor patterns. Symmetric, and 0 for identical patterns. w_j ≥ 0 lets receptors that matter for perception count more and others count less, but never backwards. It is the same distance family as the open-POM baseline recipe (finding 1); cosine is a CV alternative.
BCE (binary cross-entropy)−[ t·ln p + (1 − t)·ln(1 − p) ], summed over wordsThe standard loss for yes/no labels: each odour word either applies to a molecule (t = 1) or not (t = 0), and p is the predicted probability. Large when the model is confidently wrong.
L1 penaltyμ · Σ xAdds a cost for every bit of binding, so most x are pushed to 0 and each molecule drives only a few receptors, as real molecules do. A sparse code is easier to read and overfits less.

What each part is, why, and how it is computed (in network order)

Step 1 · molecule vector h(m) frozen

What it isA fixed list of numbers per molecule (open-POM: 256, MIST: 512) that describes the molecule in terms relevant to smell.
Why we need itThe mixture data have only 234 molecules, too few to learn chemistry. The encoder was already trained on 4,983 molecules with odour labels, so we reuse it.
IntuitionMolecules that smell alike get similar vectors.
Equationh(m) = Encoder(m)
NoteFrozen: not changed by our training.

Step 2 · binding x(m, j) learned → computed

What it isA number ≥ 0 for each molecule m and receptor unit j: how strongly m occupies j's binding sites.
Why we need itIn the nose, molecules in a mixture first compete for receptor binding sites. How strongly each one binds decides how many sites it takes, and so how much it pushes the others out.
IntuitionAlone, molecule m holds the share x/(1+x) of the sites: x = 0 → it ignores the receptor; x = 1 → half the sites; x = 4 → 80%. Large x = crowds out other molecules.
Equationx(m, j) = softplus( W_x h(m) + b_x )j
NoteW_x (R × 512) and b_x (R) are learned. softplus keeps x ≥ 0 without an upper limit (see Operations). Physically x = (c/K)n (Hill: concentration c, binding constant K, coefficient n); without concentrations we learn x directly.

Step 3 · efficacy e(m, j) learned → computed

What it isA number between 0 and 1 for each molecule m and receptor j: how strongly j fires while m sits in its sites.
Why we need itBinding and activating are different things. A molecule can occupy a receptor without switching it on (a blocker). Without e, every molecule that binds would add signal, and suppression would be impossible.
Intuitione = 1: full activator. e ≈ 0: binds but stays silent, so it only takes sites away from others. In between: partial activator.
Equatione(m, j) = sigmoid( W_e h(m) + b_e )j
NoteW_e (R × 512) and b_e (R) are learned. sigmoid keeps e in [0, 1] (see Operations).

Step 4 · receptor response r_j(A) = the hidden state computed

What it isFor mixture A, one number per receptor unit j in [0, 1): how strongly that receptor responds to the whole mixture. The list r(A) = (r_1(A), …, r_R(A)) is the hidden state of A.
Why we need itThis is where the molecules of a mixture interact. Mean or sum pooling treats them independently; the shared binding sites make them compete, as receptors do.
IntuitionEach molecule holds the share x/(1 + Σx) of the sites (the 1 is the empty share), and the response is each share times its efficacy. More molecules fill the sites (saturation, not endless growth). A strong binder with low e takes sites and adds little (suppression).
Equationr_j(A) = Σm∈A e(m, j)·x(m, j) / ( 1 + Σm∈A x(m, j) )
NoteNo weights: this step only applies the formula to x and e. The same formula gives r(B) for mixture B.

Step 5 · distance d(A, B) learned → computed

What it isThe predicted perceptual distance between mixtures A and B, in [0, 1]: the model output, compared with the target.
Why we need itThe target is a distance, so we compare the two hidden states. Not every receptor matters equally for perception, so each gets a weight. The scale and level must match how people rate (identical mixtures are rated about 0.27 apart, not 0).
IntuitionThe more the two receptor patterns differ on receptors that matter, the more different the mixtures smell.
Equationd(A, B) = sigmoid( α · √( Σj w_j² ( r_j(A) − r_j(B) )² ) + β )
Notew_j ≥ 0 (R), α and β are learned. Symmetric, and d(A, A) = sigmoid(β) for any mixture.

Step 6 · training loss training only

What it isThe number minimised when fitting the learned weights (orange).
Why we need it① the distance must match the human labels; ② 820 pairs are too few to give 64 receptor units meaning, so single molecules (4,983) must also predict their odour words from their own receptor pattern; ③ real molecules drive only a few receptors.
Intuition① fits the target, and squared error matches the RMSE score. ② keeps the hidden state about smell and trains the heads on 6× more molecules. ③ keeps the code sparse and readable, and limits overfitting.
Equationloss = meanpairs ( d(A, B) − y )² + λ · BCE( sigmoid( U r(m) ), words(m) ) + μ · Σ x
Notey = target distance. r(m) = hidden state of molecule m alone. U (words × R) is a learned readout used only in training. λ and μ are chosen by cross-validation.

O1.0: the first step, compared with B1a and O1

O1.0 keeps B1a's frozen open-POM odour words and changes only how the molecules of a mixture are combined. Each odour word is treated as one receptor. A molecule's activation of that receptor is its own word score. The molecules of a mixture compete for the receptor's sites with one binding strength per word.

Why 138: open-POM's output layer predicts exactly 138 odour words, the label set of its training data (GS-LF: 4,983 molecules, 138 descriptors). We take those 138 outputs as they are. So O1.0 has 138 receptors, one per word, and adds no units of its own.

Contributions (O1)

  1. A mixing layer with a biophysical form. Molecules combine by competitive binding with separate affinity and efficacy, learned from molecular structure and trained on perceptual mixture distance. Other DREAM models combine molecules by mean, weighted sum, attention or tree features. Novelty still to be checked against the literature before it is claimed externally.
  2. An interpretable hidden state. Each mixture becomes a receptor-like pattern tied to odour words, and the distance splits per receptor (w_j·|Δr_j|). So we can say which units, and which odour words, make two mixtures differ.
  3. A design for small data. The encoder is frozen and the model has about 75k parameters. The receptor heads are also trained on 4,983 single molecules. Mean and sum pooling are special cases, so each step is compared to the baseline by changing one thing.
  4. Strict evaluation. Choices are made by zero-overlap CV, with pairs sharing no molecule as the main metric. V50 and T46 are scored once. PLAN.md's other contributions (noise ceiling, conformal intervals, active learning) build on O1.

2 · Intuition

Receptors, not words

The nose reads a mixture through about 400 receptor types, and each molecule binds several of them. O1 compares mixtures in a receptor-like space. The odour readout [5] keeps each unit meaningful, so a difference between two mixtures can be traced back to receptors and then to odour words.

Competition is the mixing rule

All molecules in a mixture share the denominator 1 + Σx. A receptor that is already occupied does not respond more when another molecule is added. That gives saturation: large mixtures converge, as in "olfactory white". A molecule that binds strongly but activates weakly takes binding sites from the others, which gives suppression.

Mean and sum are special cases

When binding is weak (Σx ≪ 1), rj ≈ Σ x·e, the additive sum. When binding is strong (Σx ≫ 1), rj ≈ an affinity-weighted mean of efficacies, close to the mean pooling of our baselines. O1 learns where each receptor sits between the two, and the learned x values show which regime it uses.

Worked example: suppression

Receptor R1, molecule m1 alone: x = 2, e = 1 → r = 2 / 3 = 0.67.

Mixture {m1, m2}, with m2: x = 4, e = 0.1 (binds strongly, activates weakly): r = (2 + 0.4) / (1 + 6) = 0.34.

m2 halves the response even though it barely activates the receptor itself. A mean or sum of m1 and m2 cannot produce this; attention can learn something similar, but without the physical constraint.

Assumption: equal concentrations, because the training mixtures are intensity-matched and no concentrations are given. x then means "binding strength at that concentration".
